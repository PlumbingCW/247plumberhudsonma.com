Drain, waste and vent basics

Every fixture needs a drain, a trap and a vent. A vent lets air into the system so water drains without gurgling. A new bathroom added far from the existing stack may need a new vent through the roof or an approved air admittance valve where the code allows.

We plan these routes before we cut so you know where walls and floors will be opened.

Basement bathrooms and ejector pumps

A basement below the sewer line cannot drain by gravity. It needs a sewage ejector pump in a sealed pit. The pump lifts waste to the main drain. It is a component that needs a proper vent and a check valve, and it needs periodic service.

Sump pumps are different from ejector pumps. Hudson does not allow sump pump discharge into the sanitary sewer. See sump pump service.

Working with contractors and designers

If you have a general contractor, we coordinate rough-in with the framer and the inspector. We need fixture specs before we start, since the rough-in height and the drain location depend on the toilet, tub and vanity you chose.

Bring the fixture sheets to the first visit. It prevents rework.

Permits and inspections

New plumbing needs a permit from the Hudson Building Department at 78 Main Street. The Town inspects the rough-in before the walls are closed, and again at the final. Work without a permit can complicate a sale and an insurance claim.

We pull permits for the work we do and schedule the inspections.

Planning the rough-in before you buy fixtures

The order of work matters. Pick the toilet, tub and vanity first, since rough-in dimensions depend on them. A wall-hung toilet needs a carrier in the wall. A freestanding tub needs a floor drain in an exact spot. A double vanity needs two supplies and a larger drain.

Tell us about future plans too. If you may add a second bathroom later, we can leave a capped branch and a vent path. That costs little now and avoids opening finished walls later.

We also look at the water supply. A house with a small old service line or a weak water heater can struggle to feed a new bathroom. We tell you early if the supply side needs an upgrade, so the budget is honest from the first visit.

Laundry, outdoor and utility hookups

A washing machine needs two supply valves, a standpipe with a trap and a drain sized for the flow. Many older houses have a laundry tub that drains too slowly for a modern machine, so we check the drain first. Use braided stainless hoses and replace them every few years.

For outdoor water, a frost-proof hose bib runs through the wall with its shutoff inside the heated space. Close the interior valve and drain the line before winter. A garden hose left attached can trap water and split the pipe.

For utility sinks, mop basins and a hookup for a dehumidifier or a boiler, tell us what you plan to connect. Each appliance has its own drain and supply rules, and we want the permit to describe them correctly.

Codes behind the permit

New plumbing is built to the standard in the Massachusetts Uniform State Plumbing Code, 248 CMR 10.00, and gas piping to the Massachusetts Fuel Gas Code, 248 CMR 4.00. The state regulation, 248 CMR 3.05, says no plumbing or gas work may be installed, altered, removed, replaced or repaired until the inspector issues a permit, and that most permitted work is inspected before it is covered.

An inspector may ask for a rough inspection and a final inspection, and the licensed plumber must tell the inspector when the work is ready.
